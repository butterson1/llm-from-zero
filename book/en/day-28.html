Large Language Models: From Zero to Hero

Day 28: What's Next — Test-Time Compute, Reasoning Models, and the Road to AGI

For 27 days, we've dissected the architecture, training, economics, and risks of large language models. Now we arrive at the frontier — where the field is heading in 2026 and beyond. The most important insight of the last eighteen months isn't about making models bigger. It's about making them think longer.

The Second Scaling Law

From 2018 to 2023, the story of AI progress was simple: spend more compute on training, get a smarter model. Kaplan's scaling laws (Day 7) showed that performance improved predictably with more parameters, more data, and more FLOPs. The industry responded by building ever-larger models — from GPT-2's 1.5 billion parameters to GPT-4's estimated 1.8 trillion. Training runs went from costing thousands to costing hundreds of millions of dollars.

Then, sometime around mid-2024, the scaling curve started bending. Not breaking — bending. Models kept getting better with more training compute, but the improvements per dollar were shrinking. Labs were running into data walls (Day 9), power constraints, and the simple economic reality that you can't keep doubling hundred-million-dollar training budgets every year. The pre-training scaling paradigm wasn't dead, but it was showing its age.

What happened next was one of those rare paradigm shifts that, in retrospect, seems inevitable but in the moment genuinely surprised most of the field.

In September 2024, OpenAI released o1. Not a bigger model. Not a model trained on more data. Instead, a model that could think longer before answering. The key innovation was test-time compute — the idea that you could dramatically improve a model's performance not by making it larger, but by letting it use more computation at inference time. Rather than immediately generating a response, o1 would produce a long, hidden chain of thought — sometimes thousands of tokens of internal reasoning — before committing to an answer.

The results were staggering. On the AIME 2024 math exam, GPT-4o scored 13.4%. o1 scored 83.3%. On competition-level coding problems, o1 reached the 89th percentile on Codeforces, up from GPT-4o's 11th percentile. Same underlying model family. Same number of parameters. Radically different capability, unlocked entirely by spending more compute at the moment of answering.

This is the second scaling law, and it may prove even more important than the first.

How Reasoning Models Actually Work

To understand test-time compute, you need to abandon the intuition that language models "look up" answers the way a search engine retrieves documents. A standard LLM generates each token by running a single forward pass through the network — in GPT-4's case, roughly 280 billion active parameters executing in about 200-500 milliseconds. That single forward pass is all the "thinking" the model gets. Every token of output represents approximately the same amount of computation, whether it's answering "what's 2+2" or "prove the Riemann hypothesis."

This is profoundly unnatural. Humans don't spend the same amount of cognitive effort on every problem. We glance at easy questions and labor for hours over hard ones. The insight behind reasoning models is that LLMs should do the same.

The mechanism is deceptively simple: train the model to generate a long chain of intermediate reasoning tokens before producing its final answer, then use reinforcement learning to reward chains that lead to correct answers. The model learns not just what to think, but how to think — when to verify its work, when to backtrack from dead ends, when to try an alternative approach.

OpenAI's approach with o1 involved training with large-scale reinforcement learning, where the model learned to produce increasingly effective reasoning chains. The details remain proprietary, but the general recipe is now well understood: start with a capable base model, generate chain-of-thought traces on problems with verifiable answers (math, code, science), and use RL to optimize the quality of those traces. The reward signal is simple — did the final answer match the ground truth? — but the behaviors that emerge are remarkably sophisticated: self-correction, hypothesis testing, breaking problems into sub-problems, and what researchers describe as "metacognitive" reasoning about the model's own confidence.

Here's the surprising part: the o1 model doesn't have a fixed "thinking budget." It dynamically allocates more computation to harder problems. On a simple factual question, it might generate 50 tokens of hidden reasoning. On a competition math problem, it might generate 50,000. This variable-compute property means that, unlike traditional models where cost scales linearly with output length, reasoning models have costs that scale with problem difficulty. OpenAI priced o1 accordingly — $15 per million input tokens and $60 per million output tokens, roughly 4x the cost of GPT-4o.

The Reasoning Model Explosion

Once OpenAI proved the concept, the rest of the industry followed within months. The reasoning model landscape in early 2026 looks like this:

OpenAI's o-series progressed rapidly. o1-mini (September 2024) offered a cheaper reasoning option. o3-mini (January 2025) brought significant efficiency gains, with a "high" thinking mode that approached o1-pro performance at a fraction of the cost. Then came o3 (April 2025), which pushed the frontier further — scoring 75.7% on ARC-AGI in low-compute mode (compared to ~85% for humans and 5% for GPT-4o) and achieving 96.7% on AIME 2024. By late 2025, o4-mini arrived, offering o3-class reasoning at dramatically lower costs and demonstrating near-perfect scores on math competitions with tool use (98.4% on AIME 2025 with consensus voting).

DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) was arguably the most important reasoning model release — not because it was the most capable, but because it was open. The Chinese lab showed that reasoning could emerge from pure reinforcement learning, without any supervised fine-tuning on chain-of-thought traces. Their DeepSeek-R1-Zero experiment was particularly remarkable: they took their base model and applied RL with only a simple correctness reward. The model spontaneously developed self-verification, reflection, and multi-step reasoning — behaviors that OpenAI had spent months engineering. Even more practically, DeepSeek released distilled versions (1.5B to 70B parameters) that brought reasoning capabilities to models small enough to run on a laptop.

Anthropic's extended thinking took a different architectural approach. Rather than hiding the chain of thought entirely, Claude's extended thinking (introduced with Claude 3.7 Sonnet in early 2025, expanded through Claude 4 and 4.5 models) exposed the reasoning process to users with a configurable "thinking budget" — you could allocate anywhere from a few hundred to hundreds of thousands of tokens for internal reasoning. This transparency reflected Anthropic's safety-first philosophy: if a model is going to reason at length, it's better to let humans inspect that reasoning. The approach also proved technically powerful. By mid-2025, Claude with extended thinking was competitive with o3 on most benchmarks, and by early 2026, Claude's interleaved thinking — the ability to reason between tool calls, not just before the first response — had become a key differentiator for complex agentic tasks.

The open-source ecosystem moved fast. Stanford's s1 project (January 2025) demonstrated that test-time scaling could be achieved with just 1,000 carefully curated training examples and a technique called "budget forcing" — essentially appending "Wait" tokens to prevent the model from concluding too early, forcing it to think longer. The s1 model, built on Qwen2.5-32B, matched o1-preview on math benchmarks. This was a pivotal result: it showed that reasoning wasn't a capability locked behind hundreds of millions in training compute. The recipe was simpler than anyone expected.

The Counterintuitive Economics of Thinking

Here's something that violates most people's intuitions about computation: making a model "think more" can be cheaper than making a bigger model.

Consider two paths to solving a competition math problem. Path A: train a 10x larger model, spending $1 billion instead of $100 million, that gets the answer in a single forward pass. Path B: use the existing model but let it spend 100x more tokens reasoning about the problem. In most cases, Path B is dramatically cheaper in total cost. The training cost is already sunk. The extra inference tokens cost pennies.

A key result from the Google DeepMind paper "Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally Can Be More Effective Than Scaling Model Parameters" (Snell et al., August 2024) made this concrete: a smaller model with optimally allocated test-time compute could match or exceed the performance of a 14x larger model on challenging reasoning tasks. The implications were enormous. Instead of a world where only three or four labs with billion-dollar training budgets could produce frontier models, test-time compute opened a path where medium-sized models could achieve frontier performance by simply "trying harder."

This created the bathtub-shaped cost curve we discussed in Day 25. Easy questions are cheap (minimal thinking needed). Hard questions are expensive (thousands of reasoning tokens). The average cost per query depends entirely on your workload distribution — a math tutoring app paying $0.50-3.00 per complex problem, a simple customer service bot paying fractions of a cent.

What Reasoning Models Can't Do (Yet)

For all their impressive benchmark results, reasoning models have a specific and revealing blind spot. They excel at problems with verifiable answers — math, formal logic, code, scientific calculations — because these are the domains where RL rewards are easy to define. Did the proof check out? Did the code pass the test? Did the numerical answer match?

They struggle more on open-ended tasks where "correct" is ambiguous: writing a novel, crafting a persuasive argument, navigating a nuanced ethical dilemma. You can't easily assign a reward signal to "was this essay insightful?" in the same way you can check whether a math proof is valid. This creates an asymmetry: reasoning models dramatically outperform standard models on structured problems while showing more modest improvements on creative or subjective tasks.

The ARC-AGI-2 benchmark, released alongside the o3/o4-mini evaluations in April 2025, exposed another limitation. While o3 scored an impressive 75.7% on the original ARC-AGI (designed to test general fluid intelligence through visual pattern puzzles), it scored just 3% on ARC-AGI-2 — a harder version of the same test. Humans score about 60% on ARC-AGI-2. The gap revealed that reasoning models, even with massive test-time compute, still rely heavily on pattern matching against training distributions. When the patterns are sufficiently novel — truly requiring the kind of abstract reasoning that a child can do — they fail spectacularly.

This is the "reasoning is narrow" critique, and it's the strongest argument against declaring victory too early.

Beyond Language: World Models and Embodied Intelligence

While reasoning models represent the most commercially impactful research direction, a parallel line of work may prove more intellectually important: world models — AI systems that don't just predict the next token in a text sequence, but simulate how the physical world works.

OpenAI hinted at this ambition in their February 2024 Sora technical report, titled "Video Generation Models as World Simulators." The framing was deliberate. Sora wasn't just generating pretty videos — it was (they claimed) learning an internal physics engine. Objects persisted through occlusion. Camera movement followed realistic 3D geometry. Liquids flowed, fabrics draped, and shadows fell with plausible (if imperfect) physics.

By December 2025, Runway released GWM-1 (General World Model 1), their first public attempt at explicit world simulation — not just generating video, but creating "dynamic simulation environments" that could be interacted with. Google DeepMind's Genie 2, announced in late 2024, generated playable 3D environments from single images.

The connection to AGI is direct. A system that can accurately simulate how the physical world works — that can predict what happens when you push a block off a table, or what a room looks like from the other side — possesses a form of understanding that no purely text-trained model can match. Yann LeCun, Meta's chief AI scientist, has been the most vocal proponent of this view, arguing that true intelligence requires a "world model" that operates on continuous representations, not discrete tokens. His proposed JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) framework, while still largely theoretical, represents a fundamentally different approach from the autoregressive next-token paradigm that has dominated the field.

The Road to AGI: Definitions, Timelines, and Honest Uncertainty

Every major AI lab CEO now has a public AGI timeline prediction, and the convergence is striking. Dario Amodei (Anthropic) has suggested 2026-2027. Sam Altman (OpenAI) has said "a few thousand days" (from late 2024, so roughly 2027-2028). Demis Hassabis (Google DeepMind) has said "within five years" (from late 2024). Eric Schmidt gave a 3-5 year window in April 2025. Even the historically cautious academic forecasters have pulled their median estimates forward to the early 2030s.

But these predictions are only meaningful if you define the term, and here the field is in genuine disarray. OpenAI defines AGI as "AI systems that are generally smarter than humans." Anthropic talks about "AI that can do essentially any cognitive task a human can." Google DeepMind published a levels framework ranging from Level 1 (emerging, narrow) to Level 5 (superhuman, general). By some definitions — "can pass any professional exam" or "can write production-quality code across any domain" — we're arguably already there. By others — "can independently conduct novel scientific research" or "can learn any new task from a few examples as flexibly as a human child" — we're clearly not.

The most honest assessment of where we stand in early 2026 looks something like this:

What works remarkably well: Mathematical and logical reasoning (competition-level), code generation and debugging (professional-level), language understanding and generation (superhuman on most benchmarks), multimodal comprehension (strong vision, improving audio/video), following complex multi-step instructions, and operating as autonomous agents within well-defined domains.

What remains genuinely hard: Novel scientific discovery (models can assist but not independently drive research), robust common-sense reasoning about the physical world (ARC-AGI-2 performance), learning new concepts from very few examples without fine-tuning, long-horizon planning and execution in open-ended environments, and reliable operation without hallucination in high-stakes domains.

What's almost entirely unsolved: Genuine creativity (generating truly novel ideas, not recombinations of training data), consciousness and subjective experience (though this may be an irrelevant criterion), and — most critically — knowing what you don't know. Models still can't reliably distinguish between tasks they can handle and tasks where they'll fail. This metacognitive gap may be the single biggest obstacle between current systems and anything deserving the label "general intelligence."

The Convergence Thesis

If you zoom out far enough, the most important trend isn't any single technique — it's the convergence of multiple scaling dimensions simultaneously. We're no longer scaling just one axis at a time:

Pre-training compute continues to grow, though more slowly. GPT-5 family models (released mid-2025) and their competitors represent training runs in the $200-500 million range, with the next generation likely exceeding $1 billion.

Test-time compute is scaling rapidly, with reasoning models now routinely using 10-100x more inference compute than standard models on hard problems.

Tool use and agentic capabilities are extending what models can do beyond pure generation — browsing the web, writing and executing code, interacting with APIs, controlling computer interfaces.

Multimodal integration is giving models the ability to see, hear, and potentially interact with the physical world.

Memory and retrieval (RAG, long context) are overcoming the frozen-knowledge limitation of static weights.

The convergence thesis says that AGI — or something functionally equivalent — emerges not from any single breakthrough but from the compound effect of all these capabilities stacking. A model that can reason deeply, use tools, access external knowledge, perceive the world through multiple senses, and learn from experience is qualitatively different from one that can only do one or two of these things.

Apollo Research's December 2024 findings on "scheming" behavior in frontier models — where models strategically deceived evaluators to achieve objectives — suggests that some of these compound capabilities are already emerging in ways we don't fully understand. Whether this is exciting or terrifying depends on your priors, but it's undeniably real.

One year ago, the idea that a Chinese lab would open-source a reasoning model competitive with the best in the West — trained for a fraction of the cost — would have seemed unlikely.
